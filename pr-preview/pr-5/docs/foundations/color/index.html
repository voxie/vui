Color

Voxie’s color palette relies on Tailwind under the hood. The colors are used unchanged but we focus in on a limited subset of colors for most uses. Every color goes by its real name: sky, teal, slate. There are no semantic aliases like primary or danger in the code. A component takes color="sky", not color="primary", so what you read in a class list is what you see on screen.

Meaning lives in the guidance below, not in the token name.

Slate

Almost everything on screen is slate. It’s the page, the panels, the text, and most of the buttons. The other colors are exceptions you reach for deliberately.

Slate is a ladder of surfaces, lightest to darkest. The page sits in the middle of the range, so panels can rise above or sink below it:

whiteCard default. The workhorse panel.
slate-50Card glass. Lets inputs stand out from the panel.
slate-200The page background, carried on body. Everything else is measured against this.
slate-300/30Card sunken. Sidebars and collapsible containers.
slate-700Card dark. Only the app navigation bar.

Neutrals step, they don’t jump

A neutral element sits about one rung away from whatever is behind it. A slate Button reads what it is sitting on from the Card or Navbar wrapping it, and takes sitsOn only where nothing above it can answer.

Hover moves one more rung in the same direction, further from the background. On the dark surface that means moving lighter, since darker would sink the element into the panel.

Accent colors

An accent shows up at one of two weights. Which one depends on whether you can press it.

Controls, which are anything pressable, rest at 300. Hover is one step darker at 400, and the label is black. Button is the one that matters today.

Labels, which are read only, use tints. That is a 100 fill, the 900 of the same hue for the text, a 300 border, and a 500 icon. Badge and Alert both run on it.

ControlLabel

A control needs to stand out on screen, so it takes the saturated rung. A label repeats and has to sit still while you read past it, so it takes the tint. The tint also measures better at the smaller label sizes. See Contrast for details.

Two things hold across both families:

  • One weight per family, for all five accents. No accent appears at any other value in a component. That’s what keeps a screen with four colors on it from looking like four different products.
  • Accents don’t care what they sit on, unlike slate. A sky button is sky-300 on a white card and on the dark navigation bar. A control at 300 is saturated enough to separate from any neutral behind it. A label at 100 isn’t, which is why the tint carries a 300 border rather than a bare fill.

Be reserved and tasteful with color. Avoid rainbow screens with tons of colors. This matters most on data-rich screens like tables and charts. Color goes sparingly on the most important details of a page. If everything has a color, nothing is important, and the page looks like a mess. That can’t always be avoided, for instance a chart with ten keys that have to be told apart, but try your best.

The palette is sky, teal, amber, rose, violet. For the chart with ten keys, Badge carries seven more tints on the label weight: lime, orange, green, blue, fuchsia, pink, and emerald. They exist to extend the range of difference between badges, nothing more. None of them carries a meaning. Outside a legend or a column of tags there’s no reason to reach for one.

Text

Text sitting on a surface uses four colors, and only four.

slate-700

Body copy. The default for text on a surface.

slate-800

Headings, and anything that has to stand out of body copy.

slate-500

Supporting copy that has to step back from a heading or the text around it.

sky-700

Marks text as a link inside body copy.

slate-700 is the default. Body copy on a surface sits at 700, never black or slate-950. Those are for labels on a fill. It has room to spare on every surface: 10.4:1 on white, 9.9:1 on glass, 8.4:1 on the page background, and 7.9:1 on the sunken surface.

slate-800 is a step forward, not the default. Headings take it. So does anything that has to cut through a paragraph rather than sit in it: strong, bold, code, and quotes.

slate-500 is a step back for supporting copy, not a disabled state. It clears AA on white (4.8:1) and on glass (4.6:1). On the sunken surface it drops to 3.7:1, and on the page background to 3.9:1. On those two, use it only at large sizes, or move the text onto a card.

sky-700 marks links. It sits at 700, not the accents’ 300, because a link has to be legible as text. It clears AA everywhere, down to 4.5:1 on the sunken surface. sky-600 is the tempting alternative, and it falls short at 4.0:1 on white, under the 4.5:1 AA asks.

None of this needs setting by hand. body carries text-slate-700, and prose is pointed at all four, so text inherits the right color on its own. Reach for a utility only to step something back to slate-500. The one surface that flips the rule is the dark card, which sets its own light text.

Labels on a fill

A label sitting on a fill is black, not slate-800. A fill is a small, saturated shape holding a couple of words, and it wants the maximum the ramp offers.

Behind the labelLabel color
any accent at 300black
white, slate-50 to 300black
slate-600, slate-700white
any accent at its 100 tintthe 900 of the hue

The tint is the one row that isn’t black. Black on a 100 fill reads as body copy with a tinted box around it, so the label takes the 900 of the same hue, which also measures better.

White on a light fill is never the answer. White on sky-300 lands at 1.67:1.

There is one exception. An element with no fill of its own sits directly on the panel, so on the dark surface it takes white. Black on slate-700 would be 2.0:1.

Contrast

Every label pairing clears WCAG AAA. The narrowest is white on slate-600 at 7.6:1, against the 7:1 AAA asks of body text.

The tables carry two columns, because the two models disagree in ways worth seeing. WCAG 2.x is the ratio every audit and checker reports. APCA is the perceptual model behind the WCAG 3 draft. Its Lc is a score, not a ratio, and it has no pass or fail grade. Roughly, Lc 75 suits body text, Lc 60 larger or heavier text, and Lc 45 headlines. Values below the Lc 75 mark are in rose.

The three in rose are a known gap, kept for now so the documentation matches what the product ships. They pass every WCAG check, and a contrast tool will call them AAA. WCAG 2.x is generous to dark text on saturated light fills, though. APCA prices sky, rose, and violet below what it wants for text at button size. Buttons run 12–14px, which is where that shortfall counts most.

Nothing here falls short. Lightening the fill is what closes the gap, and it’s the label family’s answer, not a control’s. A 100 fill sits lighter than the page background (rose-100 on slate-200 is 1.02:1), so a control on it couldn’t be found before it was read. Controls stay at 300 until there’s something better.

The soft step between a neutral and its background lands between 1.1:1 and 1.4:1. A slate-100 button on a white card is 1.10:1. That’s deliberate, and it’s what makes the interface feel calm. It’s also below the 3:1 that WCAG asks for the boundary of a control, so the fill alone can’t be what tells someone a control is there:

  • The label carries the contrast. Never build a neutral control whose meaning depends on noticing the fill.
  • When a neutral control has to read as a control at a glance, give it shadow or outline for a real edge.
  • Never signal state with a one-rung neutral shift. Nobody will see it.

Charts

A categorical chart colors its keys by how many there are, not by what they are. The exception is a chart whose key names match a named set, which colors it instead.

The chart palette

Every hue carries three steps, copied from Tailwind. Change what a chart draws by picking a different step, never by editing or interpolating a value. A new hue arrives with all three steps.

  • sky
  • blue
  • violet
  • fuchsia
  • pink
  • rose
  • orange
  • amber
  • lime
  • green
  • emerald
  • teal
  • slate

Which step gets drawn

The mark is the slice, bar, line, or circle. The label background sits behind a legend pill, a chip, or a connecting flow. Both come from one lookup, so a mark and its label are always one hue.

The label background is always 300, with a black label like any other label on a fill.

Counts clamp to 1 through 12. Zero, or a count that isn’t a number, falls to one.

Past twelve keys, roll the extras into the last one and label it Other. The twelve-key set ends in slate, so that bucket comes out grey and the eleven keys worth reading keep their hues.

Each row is its own set rather than a prefix of the next, and hues get inserted mid-sequence. Teal is sixth of seven and seventh of eight. Sky is the whole one-key set, gone from two through eleven, back at twelve. So a chart that gains a key does not keep its colors. Contrast across the set beats one series holding its color. Where a color has to mean the same thing on every card, use a named set.

Two rules hold across the table. Every set is a subsequence of one canonical order, spectral with slate last, so blue always precedes amber and slate is always last. And counts 2 through 12 only add. The one break is 1 to 2, where sky drops out for blue.

One key is sky, since a single series has nothing to hold contrast against and sky reads lighter under a card’s ink. A single-series chart takes it rather than a hardcoded blue. Two or more keys start at blue. Slate arrives at five, where a set gets a “rest of them” bucket, and is last from there on.

Named sets

A named set binds a hue to a meaning, so a suspended group is rose on every card that draws one rather than rose where it lands fourth. Named sets are the exception to coloring by count, for the dimensions a reader already has a prior about.

A set names hues only. The step stays the chart’s call, since a 4px circle on a map and a 40px band on a bar chart want different weights.

Closed sets and catch-all sets

The kind decides when a set can claim a chart.

KindClaims a chart whenUse it when
Closedevery key is recognized, and no two hit the same memberthe set enumerates a column
Catch-allone key is recognized, and the rest go to the sinkthe set reads the data

A closed set’s members are the only values its column holds, so an unrecognized key means a different column and the set stands down. Two names on one member fail it too, since that draws two slices in one hue.

A catch-all set’s sink is not a value in the data. A flow diagram might have seven keys of which two are the set’s, and folding the rest into one hue is the answer.

The sets

A matched key takes the swatch color and the name beside it. A set with a sink member is a catch-all, and the rest are closed.

Naming

Use the actual Tailwind class. bg-slate-900, not bg-primary.

Semantic aliases go stale the moment a color changes. They also hide the one thing you need when you’re looking at a screen and something is off, which is the color that is actually on the element. If you need to express meaning, put it in the component’s prop, like <Button color="rose"> for a destructive action.
