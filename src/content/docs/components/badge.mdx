A badge labels the thing next to it: a status, a count, a category. It is a
pill of text and nothing else. It doesn't respond to hover and it isn't
clickable, so anything you can press is a [Button](/docs/components/button),
not a badge.

A badge is the main thing that takes the **label** weight in
[Color](/docs/foundations/color) rather than the 300 rung a button rests at:
a 100 fill, a 900 label, a 300 border, and a 500 icon. Badges repeat, a
column of them down a table or a row of tags on a contact, so they have to
sit quieter than a control. The tint also reads better at the 10–12px a badge
is usually set at. The [Contrast](#contrast) section below has the numbers.

## Examples

### Color

The `color` prop sets the tint: `sky` (the default), `teal`, `amber`, `rose`,
`violet`, or `slate`.

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
    <Badge>sky</Badge>
    <Badge color="teal">teal</Badge>
    <Badge color="amber">amber</Badge>
    <Badge color="rose">rose</Badge>
    <Badge color="violet">violet</Badge>
    <Badge color="slate">slate</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

The meanings are the same from the [Color Docs](/docs/foundations/color): teal for
success or completion, amber for caution, rose for failing or destructive,
sky for the primary thing on the screen, and violet as an accent with no
status attached. Slate is the one with no meaning at all, and it should stay
the answer for most badges. A colored badge in every row of a table spends
the color budget on something nobody is reading.

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
    <Badge color="slate">Draft</Badge>
    <Badge color="slate">Scheduled</Badge>
    <Badge color="teal">Completed</Badge>
    <Badge color="amber">Paused</Badge>
    <Badge color="rose">Failed</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

Because badges carry a fill and a border of their own, they hold the same
values on every surface, so there's no `surface` prop the way there is on
Button. Every surface a badge lands on is a light one. The dark card is the
app navigation bar, and nothing in there is badged.

      <Badge color="teal">teal</Badge>
      <Badge color="slate">slate</Badge>
    </div>
  </Card>

    <div class="flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
      <Badge>sky</Badge>
      <Badge color="teal">teal</Badge>
      <Badge color="slate">slate</Badge>
    </div>
  </Card>
  <Card surface="sunken">
    <div class="flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">

### Extending the range

Seven more tints sit outside the palette: `lime`, `orange`, `green`, `blue`,
`fuchsia`, `pink`, and `emerald`. They run on the same tint as everything
else, so nothing about the treatment changes.

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
    <Badge color="lime">lime</Badge>
    <Badge color="orange">orange</Badge>
    <Badge color="green">green</Badge>
    <Badge color="blue">blue</Badge>
    <Badge color="fuchsia">fuchsia</Badge>
    <Badge color="pink">pink</Badge>
    <Badge color="emerald">emerald</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

Use them only where you need to extend the range of difference between
badges. A chart legend whose keys are badges needs as many distinguishable
values as the chart has series. A table column of tags, where a team has
defined thirty of their own, needs the same thing. In both cases the job is
telling one badge from the next, not saying what any of them means.

Everywhere else, use the accents. These seven carry no meaning, and next to
an accent they borrow one they don't have: emerald beside teal reads as a
second kind of success, and orange beside amber reads as a worse warning.

#### Reaching for them in order

Thirteen tints at the same lightness is less range than it sounds. Taken in
this order, each color is as far from the ones already on screen as the set
allows:

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-2">
    <Badge size="xs">sky</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="teal">teal</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="amber">amber</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="rose">rose</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="violet">violet</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="lime">lime</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="orange">orange</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="slate">slate</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="green">green</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="blue">blue</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="fuchsia">fuchsia</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="pink">pink</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs" color="emerald">emerald</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

The separation thins out fast. By the eighth color each new tint is about a
quarter as far from its nearest neighbour as the second was, and the last few
pairs read as the same color at badge size: emerald and green, teal and
emerald, pink and fuchsia, rose and pink. Past eight categories, reconsider
whether color is carrying the distinction at all. A label, an icon, or a
sorted order will do it better than a fourteenth green.

### Size

The `size` prop controls height, padding, and text size: `2xs`, `xs`, `sm`,
`md` (the default), `lg`, or `xl`.

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
    <Badge size="2xs">2xs</Badge>
    <Badge size="xs">xs</Badge>
    <Badge size="sm">sm</Badge>
    <Badge size="md">md</Badge>
    <Badge size="lg">lg</Badge>
    <Badge size="xl">xl</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

`xs` is the size to reach for inside something dense, like a table row, a
list item, or a card header, where a taller pill pushes the line around it
apart. That's most badges in the app. Drop to `2xs` for a badge riding alongside
small text, and go past `md` only when the badge is the subject rather than
an annotation.

### Icons

Icons go in the slot next to the label. They don't inherit the label's size:
they're level with it at `2xs` and `xs`, then step one rung up the type scale
from `sm` on, so they don't turn weedy next to the text. They also take the
500 of the badge's color rather than the 900 the label uses.

<Card>
  <div class="not-prose flex flex-wrap items-center gap-3">
    <Badge color="teal"><i class="fa-solid fa-circle-check"></i>Delivered</Badge>
    <Badge color="amber"><i class="fa-solid fa-clock"></i>Pending</Badge>
    <Badge color="rose"><i class="fa-solid fa-triangle-exclamation"></i>Failed</Badge>
    <Badge color="slate"><i class="fa-solid fa-tag"></i>VIP</Badge>
  </div>
</Card>

That 500 is a decorative weight, not a legible one. On its own tint it runs
1.9:1 to 3.7:1, which is below anything WCAG asks of text. So the icon can
reinforce the label but never replace it. An icon with no label isn't a
badge; it's an icon in a pill, and a plain icon would do the job better.

## Contrast

The tint pairing is the strongest label contrast in the system. Every color
clears WCAG AAA and APCA's
Lc 75 mark for body text, which the 300-level fills on Button do not.

| Fill        | Label       | WCAG   | APCA   |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------ | ------ |
| sky-100     | sky-900     | 8.2:1  | Lc 82  |
| teal-100    | teal-900    | 8.4:1  | Lc 83  |
| amber-100   | amber-900   | 8.2:1  | Lc 83  |
| rose-100    | rose-900    | 8.0:1  | Lc 78  |
| violet-100  | violet-900  | 9.3:1  | Lc 83  |
| slate-100   | slate-900   | 16.3:1 | Lc 98  |

That margin matters more here than on anything else in the system. Badge text
runs 10–12px, the size at which a contrast shortfall counts most, and a badge
is often the only colored thing in a table row. Color has the tint set
against the control rung if you want the comparison.

The extended set lands in the same band:

| Fill        | Label        | WCAG  | APCA  |
| ----------- | ------------ | ----- | ----- |
| lime-100    | lime-900     | 8.0:1 | Lc 84 |
| orange-100  | orange-900   | 8.2:1 | Lc 82 |
| green-100   | green-900    | 8.3:1 | Lc 84 |
| blue-100    | blue-900     | 8.5:1 | Lc 80 |
| fuchsia-100 | fuchsia-900  | 8.7:1 | Lc 82 |
| pink-100    | pink-900     | 8.2:1 | Lc 80 |
| emerald-100 | emerald-900  | 8.5:1 | Lc 83 |

### What the tint costs

The tint buys its label contrast by giving up separation from the surface: a
100 fill sits lighter than the page background, and rose-100 on slate-200 is
1.02:1. That's the reason the 300 border is part of the treatment rather than
something you turn on. Without it a badge has no edge at all, so there's no
option to drop it.

| Element     | On white | On the page background |
| ----------- | -------- | ---------------------- |
| sky-100     | 1.15:1   | 1.07:1                 |
| teal-100    | 1.13:1   | 1.09:1                 |
| amber-100   | 1.11:1   | 1.11:1                 |
| rose-100    | 1.20:1   | 1.02:1                 |
| violet-100  | 1.19:1   | 1.04:1                 |
| slate-100   | 1.10:1   | 1.12:1                 |
| sky-300     | 1.67:1   | 1.35:1                 |
| rose-300    | 1.92:1   | 1.56:1                 |
| slate-300   | 1.48:1   | 1.21:1                 |

The border roughly doubles the separation, and it's still soft. Nothing here
reaches the 3:1 WCAG asks for the boundary of a control. That's fine for a
badge, which isn't a control and isn't announcing itself. It is not fine as a
way to carry meaning: as everywhere else in the system, the label does that.
Never build a badge whose meaning depends on someone noticing the fill.
