We use Tailwind's palette as-is and call colors by their real names — `sky`,
`teal`, `slate`. There are no semantic aliases like `primary` or `danger` in
the code. A component takes `color="sky"`, not `color="primary"`, so what you
read in a class list is what you see on screen.

Meaning lives in the guidance below, not in the token name. That way a
button's color can change without a name that lies about it.

## Slate

Almost everything on screen is slate. It's the page, the panels, the text,
and most of the buttons. The other colors are exceptions you reach for
deliberately.

Slate reads as a ladder of surfaces stacked from lightest to darkest, with
the page sitting in the middle of the range so panels can rise above it and
sink below it:

### Neutrals step, they don't jump

A neutral element sits about one rung away from whatever is behind it. That
is the whole rule, and it's why [Button](/docs/components/button) takes a
`surface` prop — a slate button has no fixed value, only a distance from its
background.

| Behind it    | Neutral element | On hover  |
| ------------ | --------------- | --------- |
| white        | slate-100       | slate-200 |
| slate-50     | slate-200       | slate-300 |
| slate-300/30 | slate-300       | slate-400 |
| slate-200    | slate-300       | slate-400 |
| slate-700    | slate-600       | slate-500 |

Hover moves one more rung in the same direction — further from the
background, not toward it. On the dark surface that means moving *lighter*,
since darker would sink the element into the panel.

## Accent colors

An accent shows up at one of two weights, and which one it takes depends on
whether you can press it.

**Controls** — anything pressable — rest at **300**, with a hover one step
darker at 400 and a black label. [Button](/docs/components/button) is the one
that matters today.

**Labels** — anything that only gets read — run on the **tint**: a 100 fill,
the 900 of the same hue for the text, a 300 border, and a 500 icon.
[Badge](/docs/components/badge) is the one that matters today.

The split isn't decoration. A control is one thing on a screen and it has to
be found, so it takes the saturated rung. A label repeats — a column of them
down a table, a row of tags on a contact — and has to sit still while you
read past it, so it takes the tint. The tint also measures better at the size
labels live at; see [Contrast](#contrast).

Two things hold across both families:

- **One weight per family, for all five accents.** No accent appears at any
  other value in a component. That's what keeps a screen with four colors on
  it from looking like four different products.
- **Accents don't care what they sit on**, unlike slate. A sky button is
  sky-300 on a white card and on the dark navigation bar. A control at 300 is
  saturated enough to separate from any neutral behind it. A label at 100
  isn't, which is why the tint carries a 300 border rather than a bare fill.

Be reserved and tasteful with color usage. We want to avoid rainbow screens with tons of colors. The is especially important with data rich screens like tables or charts. We use color sparingly to highlight the most import details on a page. If everything has a color then nothing is import and the page looks like a unicorn took a dump on it. This can't always be avoided for instance a chart with 10 keys that have to be differentiated but try your best.

The palette is these five, and the chart with ten keys is the exception it
can't cover. For that case [Badge](/docs/components/badge) carries seven more
tints on the label weight: `lime`, `orange`, `green`, `blue`, `fuchsia`,
`pink`, and `emerald`. They exist to extend the range of difference between
badges, nothing more. None of them carries a meaning, and outside a legend or
a column of tags there's no reason to reach for one.

## Text

Text sitting on a surface uses four colors, and only four.

slate-700 is the default. Body copy on a surface sits at 700 — not black, and
not slate-950, which are reserved for labels on a fill, where the text has a
colored shape holding it. It has room to spare on every surface: 10.4:1 on
white, 9.9:1 on glass, 8.4:1 on the page background, and 7.9:1 on the sunken
surface.

slate-800 is a step forward, not the default. Headings take it, and so does
anything that has to cut through a paragraph rather than sit in it — strong,
bold, code, quotes. The single rung between them is the whole point: 700 for
the copy, 800 for the things standing out of it.

slate-500 is a step back for supporting copy, not a disabled state. It's the
one color here with a real limit: it clears AA on white (4.8:1) and on glass
(4.6:1), but on the sunken surface it drops to 3.7:1 and on the page
background to 3.9:1. On those two, use it only at large sizes, or move the
text onto a card.

sky-700 marks links. Note it's 700, not the light rungs the accents use — a
link has to be legible as text, which sky-300 can't manage. It clears AA
everywhere, down to 4.5:1 on the sunken surface. sky-600 is the tempting
alternative and it doesn't: 4.0:1 on white, under the 4.5:1 AA asks.

None of this needs setting by hand. `body` carries slate-700 in the base
layer and prose is pointed at all four, so text inherits the right color on
its own. Reach for a utility only to step something back to slate-500, or
where a surface flips the rule — the dark card sets its own light text.

### Labels on a fill

A label sitting on a fill is **black**. Not slate-800, which is for text on a
surface — a fill is a small, saturated shape holding a couple of words, and
it wants the maximum the ramp offers.

| Behind the label           | Label color        |
| -------------------------- | ------------------ |
| any accent at 300          | black              |
| white, slate-50 to 300     | black              |
| slate-600, slate-700       | white              |
| any accent at its 100 tint | the 900 of the hue |

The tint is the one row that isn't black, and it follows from what a 100 fill
is: barely a shape. Black on it reads as body copy that happens to have a
tinted box around it, rather than as one object. The 900 of the same hue ties
the label to its fill, and it measures better besides.

White on a light fill is never the answer — white on sky-300 lands at 1.67:1.

One exception, and it's the reason components need to know their surface: an
element with no fill of its own sits directly on the panel, so on the dark
surface it takes white. Black on slate-700 would be 2.0:1.

## Contrast

Every label pairing clears WCAG AAA, the narrowest being slate-600 at 7.6:1
against the 7:1 AAA asks of body text.

Two columns, because the two models disagree in ways worth seeing. WCAG 2.x
is the ratio every audit and checker reports. APCA is the perceptual model
behind the WCAG 3 draft; its Lc is a score, not a ratio, and it produces no
pass/fail grade — roughly, Lc 75 suits body text, Lc 60 larger or heavier
text, Lc 45 headlines. Values below that Lc 75 mark are <span class="text-rose-700">in rose</span>.

Controls, at 300 with a black label:

| Fill       | Label | WCAG 2.x | APCA                                              |
| ---------- | ----- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| sky-300    | black | 12.6:1   | <span class="text-rose-700">Lc 75</span>          |
| teal-300   | black | 14.3:1   | Lc 82                                             |
| amber-300  | black | 14.5:1   | Lc 83                                             |
| rose-300   | black | 10.9:1   | <span class="text-rose-700">Lc 67</span>          |
| violet-300 | black | 11.3:1   | <span class="text-rose-700">Lc 69</span>          |
| slate-100  | black | 19.2:1   | Lc 100                                            |
| slate-300  | black | 14.2:1   | Lc 81                                             |
| slate-600  | white | 7.6:1    | Lc 91                                             |

The three in rose are a known gap, kept for now so the documentation matches
what the product actually ships. They pass every WCAG check — a contrast
tool will call them AAA — but WCAG 2.x is generous to dark text on saturated
light fills, and APCA prices sky, rose, and violet below what it wants for
text at button size. Buttons run 12–14px, which is where that shortfall
counts most.

Labels, on the tint:

| Fill       | Label      | WCAG 2.x | APCA   |
| ---------- | ---------- | -------- | ------ |
| sky-100    | sky-900    | 8.2:1    | Lc 82  |
| teal-100   | teal-900   | 8.4:1    | Lc 83  |
| amber-100  | amber-900  | 8.2:1    | Lc 83  |
| rose-100   | rose-900   | 8.0:1    | Lc 78  |
| violet-100 | violet-900 | 9.3:1    | Lc 83  |
| slate-100  | slate-900  | 16.3:1   | Lc 98  |

Nothing here falls short, and the two columns trade places to get there: the
tint scores *lower* than the control rung on WCAG 2.x and *higher* on APCA.
That's the disagreement between the models in miniature. WCAG 2.x rewards raw
luminance distance, which black against a saturated fill always wins. APCA
prices how the pairing actually resolves at small sizes, where a 900 on its
own tint holds and black on 300 starts to smear.

Which is also the answer to the gap above. Lightening the fill is the fix,
and the label family already takes it — that's the whole reason a badge isn't
a button in a smaller size. What keeps controls at 300 is the cost: a 100
fill sits lighter than the page background — rose-100 on slate-200 is 1.02:1
— so it can't describe its own edge. A label answers that with its 300
border, which is enough for something you only have to read. A control has to
be *found* before it's read, and a border alone won't do that, so 300 stays
the control rung until there's something better.

The soft step between a neutral and its background is a different story. It
lands between 1.1:1 and 1.4:1 — a slate-100 button on a white card is 1.10:1.
That's deliberate and it's what makes the interface feel calm, but it's below
the 3:1 that WCAG asks for the boundary of a control. So the fill alone can't
be what tells someone a control is there:

- The label carries the contrast. Never build a neutral control whose meaning
  depends on noticing the fill.
- When a neutral control has to read as a control at a glance, give it
  `shadow` or `outline` for a real edge.
- Never signal state with a one-rung neutral shift. Nobody will see it.

## Naming

Use the actual Tailwind class. `bg-slate-900`, not `bg-primary`.

Semantic aliases go stale the moment a color changes, and they hide the one
thing you need when you're looking at a screen and something is off: which
color is actually on the element. If you need to express meaning, put it in
the component's prop — `<Button color="rose">` for a destructive action —
where the guidance above gives it a shared reading.
