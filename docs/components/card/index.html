Card

A card is the panel most of the interface is built from. The surface prop says where the panel sits in the stack, and the card announces that to everything inside it. A Button in a card takes the right neutral without being told anything.

Examples

Surface

Content is passed as children. The surface prop sets the background. The choices are default, glass, glassRaised, layered, sunken, and dark.

Default

Most panels use this white background, including callouts, stats, dashboard panels, and list rows.

glass

Use this for content panels like settings, forms, or sidebars. Inputs stand out subtly from it, and it carries a white highlight border.

glass raised

For floating elements like sticky button containers or in-page navigation, where the blur shows as layered content slides under it. Use it only on the page background, never inside another panel. Its transparency is tuned to read as glass over the page background color. Buttons in here should follow the two button layout rule.

layered

A default card, a glass raised layer, and a sky layer, stacked at 20px offsets. Only for marketing-style callouts on a dashboard, a hero, or similar. Use it very sparingly.

sunken

Use for sidebars or collapsible containers.

dark

Only used for the app navigation bar.

The two composite surfaces pass on what a button in them can actually see. glassRaised passes on glass, and layered passes on the default white.

A surface that isn’t a card

Anything else that paints a background of its own is a surface to the eye and silent to the code. SurfaceProvider is how it speaks up. It renders one element, so it can be the box carrying the background instead of another layer around it.

<SurfaceProvider surface="dark" class="rounded-xl bg-slate-700 p-6">
  <Button color="slate">Sits on dark</Button>
</SurfaceProvider>

The navbar’s dropdown panel is the one use in the system. It is white whatever the bar is, so its buttons take slate-100 instead of the slate-600 they would inherit from a dark bar.

Reach for it only where there’s a real background. Wrapping something that paints nothing tells the buttons inside it a lie, and they have no way to check.

Padding

The padding prop sets the inset. The choices are none, compact (p-4), default (p-6), and spacious (p-8). Use none when the card wraps content that manages its own spacing, such as a table or an image.

none

compact

Used for sidebars, row items, or dense UI like a message inbox.

default

Used almost everywhere. Dashboard cards, analytics cards, form and settings cards.

spacious

For focused step-by-step flows like Quick Blast or modal content, and anywhere the main UI is faded back or information density is very low. Less content gets more breathing room.

Element

The as prop sets the tag the card renders as. It defaults to div, which is right for a card that is only a visual grouping. Switch to section when the card is a titled region of the page, or article when its content would still make sense pulled out on its own.

section

A named region of a larger page, such as a settings group or a dashboard panel with a heading.

article

Self-contained content, such as a message in a feed or a campaign summary in a list.

Under the layered surface the tag lands on the card holding the content. The offset layers behind it stay presentational.

Interaction

Cards have no hover or active styles, deliberately. A hover state would move the card off the rung its surface is documented at in Color, and it would light half the screen without saying where to aim.

When a card is meant to link somewhere or perform an action, put a Button inside it and let the button do that work. Make it the card’s primary button, so there is one obvious target instead of a panel that might be clickable somewhere. The button is also the part that takes keyboard focus and the part a screen reader announces as pressable, neither of which a card can do.
